*Tirian, age 11. His first aurora ice display. Finn is seven and has opinions about all of it.*

![[Tirian - First Light (Thumbnail).webp|450]]

---

The morning Tirian was supposed to give his first aurora display, he was in the courtyard at an hour that had no business being called morning.

The air was cold and thin at this hour, the kind that sharpened everything — the edge of the stone, the breath coming out of him in small clouds, the particular quality of his own concentration. He'd been at this for an hour already. The magic came easier than it had last week, which meant either that he was improving or that the cold helped. He didn't know which yet. He wanted to know.

A ribbon of aurora light uncoiled from his hands and dissolved three feet in front of him.

He tried again.

---

He was at it when Finn found him.

Finn was seven, which meant his operating principle was to be wherever Tirian was whenever possible, and that the hour made no difference to this calculation. He appeared at the courtyard gate in his sleeping clothes and his good boots — an odd combination — with his hair impressively disordered from sleep and the look on his face that meant he'd been awake for a while and had been waiting for a reasonable moment to come out.

"How long have you been watching?" Tirian asked, without turning.

"A while," Finn said, which Tirian suspected meant longer than he'd admit.

He let him in through the gate.

Finn came to stand beside him — not behind, not at a polite distance, but directly beside him, close enough that Tirian could feel the warmth of his aura without trying. Finn's magic had always done that: drifted outward, settled near its preferred targets. It didn't seem to require his attention. It just moved toward Tirian like water finding a slope.

"What was that one?" Finn asked, pointing to where the ribbon had dissolved.

"Practice."

"What was it supposed to be?"

"Also practice." Tirian looked at his hands. "I'm trying to find the length of it. How far it goes before it wants to come apart."

Finn considered this with more seriousness than expected. "And how far is it?"

"About three feet, right now."

"How far do you want it to go?"

"Further than that."

Finn nodded, in the solemn way he nodded when he was filing something away. "Show me again," he said.

---

He showed him again. And again after that. Finn watched all of it with the same unself-conscious attention — not performing interest, just having it. He asked good questions sometimes and strange ones other times. Did it hurt? (*No.*) Did it feel cold, from the inside? (*Sort of. More like the absence of warmth than the presence of cold.*) Was the aurora part the ice, or the light, or both?

That one Tirian actually had to think about.

"Both," he said, finally. "The ice reflects the light. And the light—" He paused. "I think the light is what decides the colors. I don't fully know yet."

"Grandmother knows," Finn said.

"Grandmother has been doing this for forty years."

"You could ask her."

Tirian didn't answer immediately. He tried again — a wider spread this time, less focused into a ribbon and more diffused, filling the space between his hands with a thin sheet of aurora-colored cold. Pink at the edges. A green that came and went. It held for five seconds before thinning out.

"You could just ask her," Finn said again, in case Tirian had missed it the first time.

"I heard you."

"So why don't you?"

Tirian closed his hands. "Because I want to figure it out."

Finn thought about this with the furrowed concentration of someone genuinely trying to find the logic. "But if she already knows—"

"Then she already figured it out. That's hers." He looked at his hands. "I want mine."

Finn was quiet for a moment, working through it — the particular quality of his silence when something hadn't quite resolved. "What if you figure out the same thing she did?" he asked finally. "Does that count?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because I did it myself."

Another silence. Finn accepted this with the easy confidence of someone who wasn't sure he understood but trusted that Tirian had a reason. "I think it's already good," he said.

"It's not good enough yet."

"Okay," Finn said, like that was perfectly fine with him. "Show me again."

---

Finn followed him through breakfast, through his morning lesson, and through the hour after that, which Tirian had planned to spend reviewing his notes on the aurora technique and which now passed with Finn in the chair across from him — occasionally reading over his shoulder or asking questions about diagrams, and occasionally just sitting there with his chin in his hand, present in the way he always was.

Their mother found them at midday and brought food she'd clearly detoured to collect on the way, which was something she did. She set it on the edge of the desk, ruffled Tirian's hair, which he accepted, and Finn's, which Finn endured with theatrical suffering.

"You're nervous," she said to Tirian.

"I'm not nervous."

"You've reorganized those papers three times since I came in."

Tirian looked down. The papers were, in fact, in a different order than they'd started.

"I want it to be good," he said.

His mother looked at him the way she looked at things when she was deciding whether to say something, which was a way she had of choosing correctly. She'd grown up without the family name, without the house behind her, without any of the careful cultivation that noble children received, and she still managed to say the right thing more consistently than anyone else he knew. Tirian had decided, some time ago, that this was not a coincidence.

"It already will be," she said.

"That's not really—"

"The people out there," she said, quieter, "most of them have never seen aurora magic. Not up close. Not like this. For them, whatever you do tonight will be the most beautiful thing they've seen in a very long time." She straightened up. "Remember that."

She left. Finn looked at Tirian.

"I told you," he said.

"You told me nothing useful."

"I told you it was already good."

"That's not the same as—" Tirian stopped. Finn was smiling at him — not the sly smile of someone who'd scored a point, but the open, easy smile of someone who had genuinely thought everything was going to be fine, and had thought so all along, and couldn't quite understand why it had taken this long to settle. "Go find something to do," Tirian said.

Finn didn't move.

---

The House Dawnmere winter gathering had been his mother's idea originally — or rather, it had been her tradition first, back when she'd still been learning what it meant to run a noble household and had decided, without much ceremony, that one of the things it meant was opening the gates once a year when the cold set in. Common folk from the surrounding streets and the river districts, household staff and their families, anyone who wanted to come in from the cold for an evening. Sorin had agreed to it with the particular expression he wore when he'd decided something was a good idea and wasn't going to admit it.

This year, Tirian was going to perform.

It had been Auris who suggested it — quietly, to his father, a few weeks ago, in the way she still had of making decisions feel obvious. *Let the boy try. He's ready to try.* He didn't know if he agreed with the second part. But he was going to try regardless.

By the time the evening came, the back garden had filled. Lanterns had been strung along the garden walls, warm and low, and braziers set at intervals so the cold was at the edges rather than the center. The household staff had set out tables along one side — cider, bread, the end-of-autumn provisions that his mother had arranged to have more of than they needed, so that there would be plenty to spare. Children Tirian didn't recognize were already weaving between the adults. He could see a cluster of older women near the wall who'd been neighbors of the estate for years, and a family from the river district his mother knew by name, and three or four men who worked the market stalls near the eastern gate.

They'd come because the gates were open and the lanterns were lit and there was food. They didn't know what the evening held yet.

In the chair they'd carried out for her, Grandmother Auris sat with a blanket over her lap and her hands folded in it, watching him with eyes that were, tonight, entirely sharp. Her mind wandered more than it used to. But she was present for this, and Tirian felt her attention the way he felt the cold — as something that sharpened him.

Finn had planted himself closer to the performance space than anyone else, near enough to be a problem.

"Three feet back," Tirian said.

Finn stepped back three feet with the exact precision of someone who had counted.

Osric appeared at Tirian's elbow, unhurried. "Whenever you're ready," he said, which was his way of communicating that a number of people had been waiting for a while. He produced his pocket watch, checked it, and put it away without elaborating.

Tirian turned to face the garden.

He breathed.

---

The display that came out of him that evening was not what he'd planned.

What he'd planned was tighter — more structured, the ribbon technique he'd been working on all week, something that would demonstrate control. What came instead was larger, and less controlled, and nothing like a ribbon.

The aurora spread from his hands and didn't stop.

It bloomed upward in a sheet that caught the cold air and crystallized as it rose, ice forming in the space above the garden with the sound of something barely audible — a soft crackling, like frost on glass — and then the light came through it, pink first, washing across the underside of the ice like sunrise in miniature. The crystals bent it. Shattered it into smaller pieces. A dozen points of pink light scattered across the garden walls, across the upturned faces of the crowd, and Tirian pushed further because he couldn't stop now and he didn't want to.

Green came next, bleeding in from the edges of the pink, and where the two colors met they produced something in between — a shifting, uncertain color that wasn't quite either, that moved as the ice moved. Spires grew upward from the initial sheet, thin and branching, reaching maybe fifteen feet before they tapered into crystalline points that caught the lantern light and threw it back as something entirely different. A small child near the front of the crowd reached upward with both arms, trying to touch something that was ten feet above her. An old woman nearby put her hand to her mouth and kept it there.

The blue-violet came last. He hadn't been certain he could produce it — had barely managed it in practice, a pale suggestion of the color rather than the color itself — but it came now without him asking, threading up through the green like a current, and when it reached the spire tips it flared briefly, genuinely bright, and the garden went white for one held second before it settled back into the aurora shimmer.

![[Finn (Young - Aurora Eyes).webp|600]]

He let his hands drop.

The ice held. It always held longer than the magic that made it — he'd learned that in the courtyard, weeks ago, the first time he'd managed anything worth keeping. The display stayed above the garden, colors shifting as the ice shifted in the cold air, and for a long moment nobody made a sound.

Then the child at the front began to clap — small, enthusiastic, completely out of time with anything — and the crowd followed.

It was not polite applause. It was the sound of people who had not expected to see something like this and had no trained response for it. He heard laughter in it somewhere, the unguarded kind. He heard someone saying something he couldn't make out, and then someone else repeating it louder. A small boy near the back had climbed onto something to see better and was being held by the collar by whoever was responsible for him.

Tirian stood in the middle of it.

![[Tirian (Young - Aurora Display).webp|600]]

He had wanted to be good at this. He had wanted that with the focused, patient ambition he brought to everything he decided was worth wanting. He had imagined, in the abstract, what it might feel like to stand in front of people and have the thing he'd built hold together above them.

He had not quite accounted for what it would look like on their faces.

Not the nobles, not the household staff who knew him as the young master and had their own reasons to be kind — the people from the river district, the market stall workers, the family his mother knew by name. The people who had come in from the cold because the gates were open and hadn't known what to expect. They were looking up at the ice with something uncomplicated on their faces. A woman in a plain brown coat was pointing something out to the man beside her, and whatever she was saying, she was smiling while she said it. Two children who'd been in motion all evening had gone entirely still, their heads tipped back, watching the colors shift.

His mother's words came back to him. *For them, whatever you do tonight will be the most beautiful thing they've seen in a very long time.*

He understood, now, what she'd been trying to tell him. Not that the display would be enough — but that *they* were enough. That the warmth on a stranger's face in the cold was something worth building toward, something that felt different from being good at a thing, from being praised for precision. The display was already dissolving at the edges, ice crystals drifting down through the lantern light like slow snow, and the people watching held out their hands to catch them.

He walked into the crowd.

He didn't plan to. His feet moved before he made any conscious decision about it. And then people were turning toward him — a woman took his hand and said something about her daughter, how she'd never seen anything like that, and he turned to find the daughter looking up at him with enormous eyes, and he crouched to her level without thinking about it and asked what she'd thought of the green. She told him the green was her favorite. He told her it was hard to do and that she had good taste. She seemed to find this deeply satisfying.

An old man nearby said his name and then said that Auris would have been pleased, which was probably the best compliment available in this particular garden, and Tirian thanked him and meant it.

He moved through the crowd and the crowd moved around him — warmly, openly, the way people moved when they weren't performing anything. He shook hands he didn't recognize. He accepted praise with the graciousness his father had taught him and with a genuine pleasure underneath it that didn't require teaching. This was what he'd wanted when he'd been in the courtyard at an hour that had no business being called morning. Not the applause, exactly. Not the recognition. *This* — the specific texture of it, the realness of it, the fact that none of these people had any reason to tell him something they didn't mean.

Finn appeared at his elbow, navigating the crowd with the easy confidence of someone who knew nobody was going to stop him.

"That's my brother," he was telling someone nearby, with the straightforward factual pride of a person reporting important news. "He did that."

The woman he was talking to looked down at him with an amused expression. "Is he?"

"He's been practicing since before breakfast," Finn said, as though this were essential context.

Tirian put a hand on top of his head and steered him gently aside. "You're welcome," he said.

"I helped," Finn said.

"You watched."

"I watched very supportively." He fell into step beside Tirian, surveying the crowd with satisfaction, as though the display had been a joint effort and both of them could now take credit for how well it had gone. Which was, Tirian thought, more or less exactly what it felt like.

---

Later, when the crowd had thinned and the ice had finished dissolving and Osric was quietly overseeing the clearing of the tables, Tirian found his father.

Sorin was standing at the edge of the garden where the lanterns didn't quite reach, watching the last few conversations wind down. He had that look on him — the one that meant he'd been paying attention to something specific and hadn't said anything about it yet.

Tirian came to stand beside him. "We should do this again," he said. "Not just once a year. More often, maybe. Something in the spring. Something in summer when the evenings are long enough."

Sorin didn't answer immediately. He was watching an older couple make their way toward the gate, the man with his hand at the woman's back. "You're thinking about the display."

"I'm thinking about what it does. When people leave here tonight, they'll talk about it. They'll remember it came from this house." He looked out at the garden. "That means something."

Sorin was quiet for a moment. The expression on his face was not the neutral bearing he wore in public — it was the other one, the one that lived underneath it. He put a hand on Tirian's shoulder, brief and deliberate.

"Talk to Osric," he said. "He'll know what's possible."

---

Tirian was heading inside when he noticed the courtyard gate was open.

He almost walked past it. Then he stopped.

Finn was in the courtyard with his arms out and his eyes closed and a look of profound concentration on his face, which on a seven-year-old looked more or less indistinguishable from the look of someone trying very hard not to sneeze. He was still in his good coat from the evening, which he had clearly not been given permission to wear outside.

A thin ribbon of color drifted from his outstretched hands — soft and warm, more gold than pink, shifting at the edges into something that might have been green. Not ice. Not cold. It had none of the crystalline quality of Tirian's magic, none of the sharp precision. It moved like breath in cold air, dispersing almost as quickly as it formed, and Finn had no idea what to do with it.

He opened his eyes. The ribbon dissolved.

He put his hands out again.

Tirian watched him for a moment without saying anything. Finn hadn't noticed him yet — was entirely occupied with the problem in front of him, brow furrowed, making small adjustments to the angle of his hands as though that might change something. The garden was quiet. The last of the lantern light fell through the gate and caught the faint color still dissipating in the air in front of his brother.

Tirian pulled the gate closed behind him.

Finn's head snapped up. "I was—" he started.

"I know what you were doing."

Finn looked at the space where the ribbon had been, then back at Tirian. "It's different from yours."

"Yes."

"Why is it different?"

"I don't know yet." Tirian looked at his brother's hands, then at his face. "Did it hurt?"

Finn shook his head. "It felt warm. From the inside." He paused. "Like the opposite of yours."

Tirian didn't say anything to that. He thought about the way Finn's aura drifted toward him in the mornings — the warmth of it at his shoulder, the way it settled without being asked to. He thought about the ribbon of color, gold at the center, nothing like ice.

"Go to bed," he said.

Finn looked like he wanted to argue. Then he decided against it, with the pragmatic efficiency of someone who recognized when he'd already gotten away with something. He pulled his coat straight and walked past Tirian toward the house.

At the door he stopped. "I'm going to figure it out," he said. Not a question. Not quite a promise. Just a statement of fact, delivered in the same tone he'd used all day.

Tirian looked at him.

"I know," he said.

Finn went inside. Tirian stood in the empty courtyard for a moment, in the cold, in the dark, in the quiet left behind by an evening full of people. Above him, the sky was clear. No aurora — just stars, and the particular stillness of a night that had held something worth holding and was now simply itself again.
