## The First Gala

The first time he danced with Amelia Irvenest, Tirian thought he understood her completely.

She moved like she'd been raised for it, which she had. The Irvenest gala ran on chandeliers turned too bright and smiles calibrated just short of genuine. Tirian knew rooms like this. He was good at them. So was she.

Red hair swept up, jewelry without a mistake, a dress that knew what it was doing. When she smiled at him across the floor, she'd already decided he was interesting. He was flattered. He was also careful enough to note it was the smile she used for heirs.

He asked her to dance anyway.

Two weeks of galas, afternoons, the particular pleasure of someone who knows how to be charming. She was funny, sharply and specifically, and she knew the political architecture of Brittania in a way Tirian found genuinely useful. He liked her intelligence. He liked that she didn't pretend she didn't have it. He told himself the other things he was noticing were just edges. Everyone had edges.

## The Second Gala

The second gala ended the way Amelia intended.

Her room was at the top of the east wing, high enough that the city noise had gone soft. Candlelight, wine she hadn't planned to finish, the quiet of a house where everyone sensible had gone to bed. She was close enough that he could smell her perfume, something warm and deliberate, chosen like everything else about her.

"You're good at that," she said. Her voice in private ran lower than it did in company.

"At what?"

"Making a room think you're half-paying attention." She studied him the way she studied things, like a puzzle she'd mostly solved. "You're paying attention to everything."

"Is that a complaint?"

"An observation." The corner of her mouth moved. "I find it interesting."

He tucked a strand of hair back from her face. She let him. Her eyes stayed on his.

"You find a lot of things interesting," he said.

"Fewer than most people think." She stepped closer, her hand resting against his chest, fingers curling into the fabric. "Mostly I find them useful."

"And me?"

She looked up at him with the direct, unguarded look she only used when she'd decided a room was safe. It was the most honest thing about her. He almost loved her for it.

"Both," she said.

He kissed her.

She kissed back like someone who'd been deciding to for a while and was glad it was settled, her hand sliding to the back of his neck. He was thinking, in some distant register, that she was extraordinary, the specific beauty of her, how easy it was to forget, in a moment like this, what else was there. Her other hand found his lapel. His found her waist.

They broke apart. Her eyes were bright.

"You're still thinking," she said, breath short, more amused than annoyed.

"Constantly."

"Stop." She kissed him again, slower, like making a point.

He stopped thinking.

For a little while, at least.

## The Market

They were in the upper market when he first saw it.

She had stopped at a jeweler's stall, not to buy anything, Tirian suspected, but because she'd caught her own reflection in the polished case. The merchant, an older man with ink-stained hands, asked if she needed help.

Amelia glanced at him the way you glance at furniture.

"I'll call you if I do," she said, and turned back to the case.

The man went back to his work. He was used to it, clearly. Tirian watched his face and saw nothing, because there was nothing left to show.

On the way out, Tirian bought a ring from him. He didn't need it. He paid more than it was worth.

Amelia didn't notice.

## The Sitting Room

The third moment was the one that settled it.

They were in the Irvenest sitting room, waiting on afternoon tea, when Kaisel appeared in the doorway. Seventeen, training sword at his side, hair damp from the yard. There was something quieter about him than Tirian expected. Not sad, exactly. Just not quite full.

He looked like Finn. Not in the face, not in the build. Something else. The way he held the sword like it was part of his hand. The particular stillness of someone who'd recently lost something and hadn't figured out yet how to stand without it.

Tirian felt it before he could stop it. The specific pain of recognition.

Kaisel nodded at Tirian first, then looked to his sister.

"Amelia, do you know where Father—"

"You're tracking dirt." She didn't look up from her book.

Kaisel glanced at his boots. Barely muddy. He stepped back to the threshold.

"He was in the study earlier," she continued, turning a page. "Whether he's still there is not my concern."

"Right. Thank you." He started to go.

"Also, if you're going to embarrass yourself at the yard, clean up before walking through the house. Some of us have guests."

He stopped. Something moved through his face and settled back. The expression of someone who'd made a decision a long time ago and kept remaking it in small moments like this one.

"My apologies," he said. To Tirian. Not to her.

"Don't apologize," Tirian said.

Kaisel blinked. Amelia looked up.

Tirian nodded at the sword. "That a longsword?"

"Yeah."

"Good balance?"

The question sat differently in that room. Kaisel looked at the blade, then back up, and something in him shifted. Not the grin Tirian suspected was his default. Something quieter than that, but real.

"Better than most of the estate swords. I've had it a long time."

"Usually the way," Tirian said. "The ones you've had a long time fit better."

A pause. Then a small smile, and Tirian could tell it cost him something to produce it, and that he gave it anyway.

"I'll let you get back to it," Kaisel said, and left.

The room settled.

Amelia had already found her page.

"He really is insufferable after training," she said. "Gets so cheerful about it."

Tirian looked at her.

She looked back, unbothered, like nothing had been said worth commenting on.

He thought about Finn. Sixteen and brown-haired and easy with a sword. The way he'd appear in doorways just like that, damp and restless. The way he'd smiled at Tirian with a total confidence that the smile would be returned.

Tirian had returned it every time. He'd had no idea, then, what it cost nothing to give.

He set down his cup.

His mother had been a commoner. She remembered the name of every servant in every house they'd visited. The footman at the Carlyle estate. The old woman who brought the winter banquet tea. The stable boy who never looked up at anyone until Lirien looked at him first. Tirian used to think that was just who she was.

Now he understood she chose it. Quietly, over and over, until it looked like instinct.

Amelia was wonderful to him. Sharp and funny and warm when the room held people she considered worth warming to. She had never once made him feel small.

She had made Kaisel step back from a room he lived in.

She had looked through a merchant like he was glass.

Both times, without thinking. That was the whole of it. Not cruelty. Not malice. Just the practiced absence of a choice his mother made every day without effort.

His mother would have known the merchant's name.

He couldn't accept the difference.

## The Ending

He ended it three days later.

Small sitting room, east garden, late afternoon. Private enough that she wouldn't be embarrassed, contained enough that she couldn't redirect. Amelia was good at redirecting. He wanted to give her as few openings as possible.

She was at the window when he arrived, a glass of wine untouched in her hand. She turned, and he saw immediately she'd been expecting this. She'd felt the shift in him these past three days and had been waiting to find out if she was right.

"Go ahead," she said.

"I think we should end this."

She studied him the way she studied things she was deciding how to handle.

"Because of Kaisel," she said.

"Partly."

"He's—"

"I know what you're going to say."

"Then you know—"

"Amelia." Quiet. She stopped. "I watched you look through him like he wasn't there. In his own house. Then the moment he left you called him insufferable." He let that sit. "You weren't even trying to be cruel. That's what I keep thinking about."

Her chin lifted. Something quick moved through her face and went.

"So I'm being judged for not performing warmth I don't feel."

"For not feeling it."

That landed. She turned to the window, jaw tight, and pulled herself back together with the precision of long practice. Her hand found the glass. She didn't drink.

"I was good to you." To the garden. Voice careful, gone quiet. "You know that."

"I do."

"I wanted this." She stopped. Started again. "It wasn't only—" Another stop. She pressed her lips together and chose the pride. "I thought you were different from most men I've met."

"I probably am," he said. "That's the problem."

She turned back. Her eyes were bright, not with tears, but with something unresolved, something that wanted a fight and couldn't find the argument for it.

"This is about a merchant," she said. "And a boy who doesn't knock."

"It's about what kind of person I can build something with." He said it without cruelty. "I'm sorry. I genuinely am."

She looked at him a long moment, cataloguing. Then something flattened in it, and he recognized the moment she stopped hoping he'd take it back.

"Then say it cleanly," she said.

He looked at her. Beautiful, and she was, standing in the gold of the late afternoon, her expression set like a painting. She had been wonderful to him. He hoped someday she'd decide to be wonderful to someone who couldn't give her anything.

**"You inherited your mother's beauty," he said. "But clearly not her heart."**

The room went still.

Her expression didn't break. Something went out of it, the way a candle goes when you cup your hand around the flame. When it came back, a door had closed behind it.

"Get out," she said. Quietly.

He left.

Out through the estate, into the evening. He thought about his mother knowing the name of every person who'd ever handed her something. About a boy smiling like nothing was wrong, because nothing being wrong was the best he'd learned to expect.

*I hope he finds someone who sees him.*
