## Part I — One Night

"You're doing it again," Finn said.

Tirian looked up from the letter. He hadn't heard his brother come in. Finn was leaning in the doorway of the study with his arms crossed and a look on his face that meant he'd been standing there for a while, watching, and was going to make a production of this.

"I'm finishing something," Tirian said.

"You've been finishing something since after supper."

"It's correspondence. It doesn't finish itself."

Finn pushed off the doorframe and dropped onto the edge of the desk, picking up a document and squinting at it in a way that suggested he had no idea what he was reading. "House HOUSE's old border dispute," he said, reading the top line. "Tirian. The gala starts in an hour."

"I know when the gala starts."

"Do you? Because you look like a man who forgot there was a world outside this room."

Tirian set down his quill. He didn't have a good answer to that, and Finn knew it.

The letter was nothing urgent — a question of precedent in a trade negotiation, something that had been festering between two houses for longer than either party would admit. House HOUSE had signed an agreement two hundred years ago that everyone now interpreted differently. Tirian had been cross-referencing the original text against the secondary documentation for most of the afternoon. He had a position forming. He wanted to get it down before it slipped.

"Leave the books and letters at home," his father's voice came from the hall. Sorin filled the doorway with a kind of effortless authority that Tirian had spent years studying and still couldn't fully replicate. He was dressed in white and gold, the Dawnmere colors warm against his broad shoulders. He looked at Tirian and shook his head, but his eyes were fond.

"One more minute," Tirian said.

"You told Magistrate Coll he thinks three moves ahead of grown men twice his age," Finn said, turning to their father.

Sorin looked unimpressed. "I told Coll that when it was a compliment. Tonight it's a liability."

Tirian capped the ink. "If I understand why a wound is still open after two centuries, I can find a way to close it — or at least make sure it never opens on *us*. That's not idleness, Father. That's how you build something that outlasts the both of us."

Sorin looked at him for a moment. Something in his expression shifted — not disagreement, exactly. Something softer than that.

"I know," he said. "I also know that the Dawnmere seat will still be standing tomorrow morning, and tonight you are expected at a gala with people who need to see you and not a letter about a border dispute that has nothing to do with us yet."

*Yet*, Tirian thought. That was precisely the word.

But he let it go. He capped the ink. He set the papers aside.

His mother found him at the bottom of the staircase, adjusting the cuffs of his coat. Lirien had a way of appearing at exactly the right moment — it was a skill he'd once thought was magical and now suspected was simply a result of knowing her household better than her household knew itself.

"He told Coll that," she said, taking over the cuff he was fighting with and folding it flat in two efficient motions.

"I heard."

"He tells people that. He doesn't usually tell you."

Tirian looked at her. She gave him the small, closed-mouth smile that meant *you're welcome*, and stepped back to assess him.

"You look like a Dawnmere," she said, with the particular warmth that made that a statement rather than just a fact.

In the front hall, Lyra was sitting on the bottom stair with her stuffed fox in her lap — a battered, beloved thing whose left ear had been stitched back on so many times it sat at a slightly different angle than it used to. She looked up when she heard him on the stairs and offered the fox, as though presenting a document.

"Look," she said.

"I'm looking," Tirian said.

"He's come," she said solemnly. "To see you off."

"He's very dignified."

"He is." She tucked the fox under her arm and looked up at him with the full, solemn gravity she reserved for important occasions. "Bring me back something pretty."

"The gala isn't really that kind of—"

"Something pretty," she repeated.

"I'll see what I can do," he said, and crouched to pull her into a brief, tight embrace that she suffered with the long-suffering patience of a twelve-year-old who considered herself, on balance, too old for this.

"I am the *exact* right size," she said, against his shoulder.

"You are exactly the right size," he agreed.

He stood and found his father waiting at the door. Sorin put a hand on his shoulder — a rare thing, brief and deliberate.

"Let it stand without you," he said. "Just this once."

Tirian looked at him. He looked at his mother in the hall behind him, at Finn leaning against the doorframe with his arms crossed, at Lyra sitting on the stairs with her fox.

"Of course," he said.

He rode out into the evening. He did not look back.

## Part II — The Gala

The city moved like money. That was the thing about evenings like this — the way wealth organized itself into motion, channeled through streets lit with expensive lanterns, carried in carriages with polished panels and house crests done in lacquered gold. Tonight the destination was the [[House Howelle]] estate, and the occasion was the kind that made even jaded nobility lean forward: an engagement.

[[Jeane Howelle]] — the Queen's Guard, the first woman ever inducted into [[The Kingsguard]] — was to be married. Her intended was [[Lanius]], a ward House Howelle had taken in as a child from the slums of [[Caer Brittanis]] and raised alongside Jeane ever since. It had been quietly known for some time that he was a Chosen of the gods, though House Howelle had never made a show of it; to most of the city he was simply the ward, a fixture of the household, a name attached to House Howelle's at gatherings like this one without ever quite being the center of them. He had even been considered for the Kingsguard once — Tirian had heard that much — and ultimately passed over. The engagement was the surprise instead. Half the hall seemed to still be recalculating what they thought they knew about him.

The gala itself was hosted in a hall built to make the people inside it feel permanent. High ceilings. Old stone. The kind of room that made your voice carry differently.

Tirian moved through it the way he'd been taught — present, unhurried, attentive without appearing watchful. He accepted wine he didn't drink. He remembered names. He asked the right questions and listened to the answers, and when people made the kind of off-hand remarks that were actually tests, he passed them without letting anyone see that he knew he was being tested.

A daughter of one house wanted to talk about the upcoming trade summit. He spent twenty minutes with her and walked away having agreed to nothing but having left her with the impression of a young lord who understood more than his age suggested.

This was the work. Not the formal negotiations, not the signed agreements — those came later, or not at all. This was the layer before that: a small favor traded for a smaller one, a name learned, a connection opened or kept warm. The quiet, patient accumulation of understanding between people who would matter to each other eventually. Building something that would still be standing long after tonight's wine had worn off.

---

He found his friends near one of the long windows, where the noise of the hall thinned out enough to actually hear each other.

"There he is." [[Lumiere Ameris]] didn't bother lowering his voice. "Tirian Dawnmere, working the room like he's billing someone for it."

"Someone has to," Tirian said. "Lumiere."

"I've been here twenty minutes and I've already been complimented on my posture by three different mothers." Lumiere said it like a victory, because to him it was.

"Your posture is good," [[Aevos]] said. "It's the rest of you that's the problem."

"You're one to talk. You walked in an hour late and announced yourself."

"I made an entrance," Aevos said, entirely unbothered. "There's a difference."

[[Ludwig St. James]] had a glass of something he wasn't drinking either, turning it slowly by the stem. "I, for one, think it's a lovely night for an engagement," he said, with the particular innocence of a man who had spent the last hour failing to talk to a woman near the dessert table and didn't want to discuss it.

"How's that going for you, Ludwig?" Lumiere asked.

"Wonderfully."

"You stuttered."

"Once."

"Twice," said [[Damian Grimm]], without looking up from his own glass. It was the first thing he'd said since Tirian arrived, and it landed with the dry precision of someone who'd been quietly counting.

Ludwig pointed at him. "Nobody asked the bastard."

"Illegitimate," Damian corrected, mildly. "There's a difference."

That got a laugh out of all of them — even Damian, a small one, more breath than sound. Across the hall, Tirian caught sight of [[Manuel]] in his Kingsguard dress uniform, a head taller than everyone around him, and beyond him [[Kylo Wisemoon]] and [[Lucius Raymor]] standing near the dais where [[Godwyn Atreides]] was holding court with a cluster of older lords. The whole circle, more or less, all gathered under one roof for once.

"Have you congratulated the happy couple yet?" Aevos asked.

"Not yet," Tirian said.

"You should. He's over there." Aevos nodded toward the edge of the hall, where Lanius stood somewhat apart from the densest part of the crowd, a glass in his hand that he didn't seem to have touched either. "Looks like he could use the company, honestly. Half the room's still staring at him like he grew a second head."

---

Tirian found him by the tall windows, looking out at the gardens rather than at the party behind him.

"Lanius," Tirian said. "Congratulations."

Lanius turned, and something in his expression eased slightly when he saw who it was — not a stranger angling for an introduction, not someone who wanted to ask about the Kingsguard. "Tirian Dawnmere." A small nod. "Thank you. I think."

"You think?"

"It's a strange thing, having a room full of people decide you're interesting on the same night." Lanius glanced back toward the crowd. "An hour ago half of them couldn't have told you my name. Now I'm being congratulated by men who spent the last decade not looking at me."

"For what it's worth, I'd already heard your name," Tirian said. "Jean speaks well of you."

"She would." There was real warmth in it, under the dryness. Lanius was quiet for a moment. "You're easier to talk to than most of them. No offense."

"None taken. Most of them are exhausting."

That earned something closer to a real laugh. "I heard you turned down two trade proposals tonight without anyone realizing they'd been turned down."

"They weren't proposals. They were tests to see if I'd agree to something I shouldn't." Tirian shrugged. "It's not difficult once you know what to listen for."

"That's not nothing, though. Most people in this room only ever learn to listen for what's useful to them." Lanius considered him for a moment. "I heard House Dawnmere's been moving grain into the river districts. Below cost. Word is your father authorized it personally."

"My father did, yes. Though it was as much my idea as his, by the end." Tirian found he didn't mind talking about it — it was rare, at gatherings like this, for anyone to ask. "It's not charity, exactly. Or it is, but it's also just — the harvest came in heavier than usual this year, and the river districts had a hard winter. It costs us very little and it means people don't go hungry. I don't understand why that's a difficult calculation for most houses to make."

"It isn't difficult. That's what makes it strange." Lanius looked back out at the gardens. "I grew up hearing a phrase. 'It is the duty of the strong to protect the weak.' I've heard it my whole life, in this house, from people who say it like a blessing over a meal and then never think about it again. You're the first person I've met who says something like that and it actually describes what you did that week."

Tirian didn't have a ready answer for that. "I don't know if it's that simple," he said finally. "But I'd rather try and be wrong about how much it helps than not try at all."

"Yeah." Lanius said it quietly, like he was filing it away somewhere. "Yeah, I think that's right."

---

The servant appeared at Tirian's elbow a few minutes later, wearing the colors of House Howelle. He was carrying a small folded note on a tray, which was the formal way of doing it, which meant the message itself was meant to be serious.

Tirian took the note.

"Trouble?" Lanius asked, with the easy half-attention of someone who didn't expect the answer to matter.

"Probably just my father," Tirian said. "He has a talent for finding me even when I've successfully avoided everyone else."

He unfolded the paper.

He read it once.

The room did not stop. The music continued. Somewhere behind him, Lumiere said something that made Ludwig protest loudly, and Aevos laughed. A servant appeared at his elbow to offer more wine and he waved them off without looking.

He read it again.

"Tirian?" Lanius's voice, from somewhere that suddenly felt very far away.

He put the note in his coat pocket.

"Excuse me," he said — to Lanius, to the room, to the entire evening. He didn't remember afterward what his voice had sounded like when he said it, whether it had given anything away. He hoped it hadn't. He never asked.

He walked to the nearest exit in a straight line, which was the wrong way to leave a gala, but the world had already moved on without him asking it to.

## Part III — What the House Looked Like

The gate was open.

Not damaged — just open. Sitting on its hinges like someone had passed through recently and not bothered to close it behind them. In the dark, with the lanterns unlit along the drive, it looked like an absence more than an entrance.

Tirian dismounted and tied his horse at the post.

The front door was unlocked. He pushed it open.

The entry hall had been pulled apart. Not destroyed — there was nothing broken, no shattered glass, no torn curtains. It looked more deliberate than that. Drawers pulled open and left that way. A small cabinet emptied, its contents distributed across the floor in a loose scatter. A chair tipped. Papers disturbed on the side table, spread apart as though someone had rifled through them.

He stood in the doorway and looked at it.

On the side table, still sitting exactly where it always sat, was the Dawnmere seal — the golden sun, a little worn from years of use, resting in its small dish.

He noticed it. He noted that a thief would have taken it. He noted the specific way the room had been disturbed — methodical rather than frantic, staged rather than chaotic — and something in the back of his mind began a sentence it didn't finish yet.

Then grief swallowed it.

It came in like a tide — not a wave, not a crash, just the slow, certain weight of water filling a room. He was still standing in the doorway. He was still looking at the seal. He moved deeper into the house.

## Part IV — What He Found

He found his father in the sitting room.

Sorin was on the floor beside the armchair — had been moved there, or had fallen, it was hard to tell. His hands were curled at his sides, still reaching for something that wasn't there anymore. The wounds were quiet in the way that violence goes quiet when it's finished with its work. Tirian crouched beside him. He stayed there for a long time without moving.

He found his mother near the kitchen stairs. There was a knife near her hand — not her knife, she didn't carry one, she must have taken it from somewhere — and her hair was still half-pinned, the way it looked when she'd stepped away from her mirror and been interrupted. She had come toward the noise. Tirian already knew this about her. She would have come toward it.

He found Auris in the chair by the hearth. She looked like she had simply fallen asleep, except that she hadn't. Her tea was still in its cup, gone cold, sitting on the side table beside her. Her face had a bewildered quality — like the very last thing she'd understood had been that something was very wrong.

He found Finn in the doorway between the hall and the study. He was on his side, one arm stretched toward the doorframe. His fingers had made it to the edge of the wood and no further. He had been trying to reach something, or someone. He had been one stride too far away and one breath too late, and there was nothing in the world Tirian could do to change that now.

He found Lyra in her room. She was in bed, the way you might expect to find a twelve-year-old late at night — except that her hand was reaching, over the edge of the mattress, toward the floor. He looked down and found the fox. It had fallen. She had been reaching for it when everything stopped.

He sat down on the floor beside the bed. He picked up the fox. He held it.

*You told me to go. I went because you told me to go.*

He heard his father's voice: *Let it stand without you. Just this once.*

He heard his own: *Of course.*

*I should have been here. I could have stayed home. I could have stayed with my books and my letters and my treaties, exactly the way you told me not to, and I could have been here when—*

He stopped.

He turned the fox over in his hands. It was worn smooth in the places where Lyra had held it most — the base of the left ear, the belly, the small flat nose. The ear she'd stitched back on so many times sat at that slight angle. There was a small patch of stitching on the back, rougher than the original seams — she'd done it herself, maybe two years ago, when she'd decided she was old enough to mend her own things.

He thought about all the last words he hadn't said. The things left unfinished — the correspondence, the documents, the careful architecture of *tomorrow*. He had been so certain there was more time. There was always more time. That was the nature of houses, of families, of dynasties: they were built on the assumption of continuation.

*Most boys my age don't have a house to look after*, he had once said, the way you say something true that you also want to be heard as proud.

He had been so busy looking after it. He had been so certain that was the same as being here.

He realized he was crying only after he couldn't stop.

## Part V — The Weight of Morning

Morning came without his permission.

He was still in the house. He had moved at some point — couldn't have said when or why — and found himself in the entry hall again, sitting on the floor with his back against the wall. Pale light through the narrow windows. Birdsong somewhere outside.

He felt hollowed. Not empty, exactly. More like a room where someone had taken everything that made it a room and left only the walls. The grief was still there — it wasn't going anywhere — but the edge of it had gone numb the way a wound does when it's deep enough. He could breathe again. He wasn't sure that was better.

He sat there for a long time.

Then, without meaning to, he looked at the side table.

The Dawnmere seal was still there.

He stared at it. And the thought that had started the night before and been swallowed by the flood — the thought that hadn't finished — surfaced again, quiet and relentless.

*No thief left that behind.*

He stood. He walked to the table. He picked up the seal and held it.

He started moving through the room again, slowly. Not as a grieving son this time — as the person his father had been telling Magistrate Coll about. Methodical. Patient. Looking at what was actually there.

The documents on the floor. He picked through them. They were important papers — not the most important, not the ones a rival house would want — but not nothing either. A thief taking documents selectively would have taken the ones worth something. These were scattered, not curated.

The jewelry on the dressing table upstairs. He checked. Some was missing, some wasn't. What was missing was the obvious: the pieces that any household servant would know about, the ones on display. What wasn't missing was the rest. A real thief would have been more thorough, or more specific, not both inadequate and random.

The wounds, again. He made himself look. They were many, and certain, and the people who had made them had known what they were doing. Professionals didn't work this hard unless someone had paid for precision. Traffickers would have been careful to leave no trace — and a robbery gone this wrong would have left some. A rival house doing this would have started a political crisis by morning; he would have had four messages on his door before dawn. An opportunistic robbery gone wrong would have looked different — messier, more panicked, inconsistent.

None of it fit.

He stood in the entry hall again and looked at the seal in his hand.

*Someone wanted me to believe this was a robbery.*

The thought landed without drama, without revelation. Just weight. Someone had chosen the staging carefully. Had understood that a chaotic scene would be read as random, would buy time, would muddy the inquiry before it even started. Had planned for the morning after — for what investigators would see, for what conclusions they would reach.

*Someone wanted everyone to believe that.*

And they had been careful enough to succeed — or careful enough to have tried. The scene was good. The scene would have convinced most people who walked through that door.

Except that he knew this house better than any investigator. He knew what was kept where, and what was worth taking, and what a real robbery looked like versus what this looked like. He had spent years learning the shape of the world in order to see when the shape was wrong.

He'd found the wrongness. He didn't know what to do with it yet.

He stood in the morning light with his family's seal in his hand, in the silence of a house that used to be full of them, and understood something he hadn't been ready to understand the night before: this hadn't been an accident, or an act of chaos, or a terrible piece of misfortune that had landed on his family without intent.

Someone had looked at House Dawnmere and decided to end it. Someone had planned for it, paid for it, and then arranged for it to be explained away.

He was still alive.

He didn't know why.

He stood in the pale morning, grief and guilt and this terrible new knowledge filling the same space inside him at once, and found that he had nowhere to put any of it. Not yet. Not today.

He stood there anyway.
